Read the specimen first. The tells are easier to see in the evidence than in the advice.

The patterns

Three ways a scam gets in.

Short modules, built from patterns that repeat across Malaysia. Each one ends with a specimen you can read like evidence.

A quick guide

How to read these lessons

Each lesson explains a scam, shows warning signs, and teaches you how to stay safe.

  • Yellow highlight Important warning signs or details you should pay attention to.
  • The Check A simple action you can take to verify something suspicious.
  • Specimen A fictional scam message or conversation showing how a scam might appear.

  1. 01

    The login

    Understanding Phishing Attacks

    A stolen password is still the most common way an account is lost. The message borrows a bank’s colours, copies a login screen and adds a deadline — but the address is never quite right. AI has made the writing cleaner; it has not learned your bank’s real domain.

    1. The domain is a lookalike — one swapped letter, a hyphen, or .net instead of .com.my.
    2. A deadline measured in minutes, not days.
    3. You are asked to log in from a link, instead of opening the app you already have.

    The check Type the address yourself. Never log in from a link.

    Specimen analysis / 01

    Why is this email suspicious?

    1. Unverified sender

      A familiar sender name does not prove that the email came from a genuine organization.

    2. Artificial urgency

      A 30-minute deadline pressures you to act before checking the facts.

    3. Suspicious link

      The email directs you to a website that has not been independently verified.

  2. 02

    The voice

    AI impersonation

    Cloning a voice takes a few seconds of audio — a story, a voicemail greeting, a video. Pair it with a new number and a family emergency, and the scam becomes a conversation you believe you are having with someone you trust. Video calls are the same trick with a face.

    1. A new number claims to be someone you know.
    2. The call cannot be returned, or the caller refuses to be seen.
    3. Money, gift cards or a transfer — urgently, and “don’t tell anyone yet”.

    The check Hang up. Call back on the number you already have.

    Specimen analysis / 02

    Can you trust a familiar voice?

    Not necessarily. AI-generated voices can imitate someone you know. Pay attention to these warning signs:

    • An unfamiliar number

      The caller claims to be someone you know, but contacts you from a different number.

    • An urgent request

      They pressure you to send money, share a verification code, or act immediately.

    • Verify independently

      Contact the person using a number or channel you already trust. Never share a one-time verification code.

  3. 03

    The offer

    Fake job offers

    The offer arrives on WhatsApp or Instagram: flexible hours, RM300 a day, no experience needed. The work is liking videos or “verifying orders”. Then the job asks you to pay a fee, or to receive money for someone else. That is the point at which it stops being a job.

    1. You are asked to pay to start work — a deposit, a training fee, an “activation”.
    2. The employer exists only on a messaging app, with no company name, address or contract.
    3. Your bank account is used to move someone else’s money. That makes you the mule.

    The check Never pay to start a job. Never lend your account to move money.

    Specimen analysis / 03

    Why is this job offer suspicious?

    A convincing recruitment message can still contain warning signs. Look closely at the claims and requests.

    1. Unverified earnings

      High earnings promised for simple tasks should be independently verified.

    2. Upfront payment

      A request to pay before starting work is a major warning sign.

    3. Artificial urgency

      Pressure to apply immediately can prevent you from checking whether the job is genuine.
